Nigerian Military Counter-Insurgency Operating Environment

Reporting period: 7 – 14 September 2026 (7 days) | Issued: Monday 14 September 2026. Three things changed the picture this week. First, a night infiltration attack on FOB Pulka in Gwoza on 7 September was repelled with the air component providing ISR and then striking the withdrawing force — multi-source. Second, The New Humanitarian reported on 7 September that a secret three-month ceasefire between the government and the Bakura Doro faction of Boko Haram/JAS has been in force since June and is expected to be extended; ISWAP is not party to it and the government has not commented. Third, the theatre we support changed commander: Maj Gen I. A. Ajose took over Operation HADIN KAI on 9 September. The visible air campaign returned, with a Lake Chad island interdiction at Tumbunma Baba and a five-aircraft package reported over Yankuzo in Zamfara. Background from the baseline assessment and earlier issues is not repeated.

1. Bottom Line Up Front

  • FOB Pulka was attacked at night on 7 September and the air component fought the defence — ISR overhead, then precision strikes on the withdrawing party. Multi-source. At about 22:00 on Monday 7 September suspected ISWAP fighters attempted to infiltrate the forward operating base at Pulka, Gwoza LGA, on the Mandara Mountains axis. Troops held; a reinforcement team from 26 Task Force Brigade Garrison mobilised; the OPHK air component provided ISR coverage and, as the attackers broke contact, struck them to disrupt the withdrawal. The official OPHK statement (Capt Mohammed Goni, 8 September) reports one soldier wounded in the left arm; the security report obtained by Zagazola gives one attacker killed, two soldiers wounded and evacuated to 7 Division hospital, and three MRAP tyres damaged. The casualty discrepancy is minor and typical of first reports. For our purposes this is the clearest recent example of the tasking pattern that will actually reach an air unit here: a night base-defence call, ISR first, then a strike on an enemy exfiltration route — with the enemy now on notice that withdrawing from a failed base attack draws air. Pulka is the same axis as the Ngoshe hostages and sits inside the area covered by the reported ceasefire below, which makes the ISWAP attribution worth noting: it is the excluded faction that is still attacking.
  • A secret three-month ceasefire with Boko Haram/JAS has reportedly been in force since June — ISWAP is not part of it, and it changes the targeting question. The New Humanitarian reported on 7 September, citing three people with contacts inside the group, that a truce between the government and the JAS leadership under Ibrahim Bakura Doro has held since June, is linked to the release of 360 Ngoshe hostages, reportedly involved a ₦5 billion payment the government denies, and is expected to be extended for a further 40 days. Communities around Ngoshe say attacks have stopped. The government, Defence Headquarters and the Army did not respond. Two of the same sources warned JAS may be using the truce to move ‘equipment’ into the Mandara mountains. Treat it as credible but unconfirmed: single-outlet reporting, widely republished (Premium Times, Pulse, allAfrica), no official acknowledgement. Three things follow for an air unit. Positive identification between JAS and ISWAP has just become a policy problem as well as a tactical one — if a no-strike understanding exists for Bakura-aligned groups in parts of the Mandara/Gwoza belt, it has not been published, and the crews flying there need to know whether one exists. The ‘lull’ reported around Ngoshe is political, not proof of military effect, and a cover for re-supply is exactly what the sources fear. And it re-reads last fortnight’s picture: JAS–ISWAP clashes in Sambisa continue (TNH cites them as evidence the deal excludes ISWAP), OPHK’s August surrender figure may partly reflect the truce, and the attacks that continue — Pulka, Kareto–Damasak, the Lake Chad axis — are ISWAP’s.
  • The visible air campaign returned: a Lake Chad island interdiction at Tumbunma Baba (~18 claimed killed) and a reported five-aircraft package over Yankuzo, Zamfara — plus Police Air Wing strikes in Katsina and Zamfara. OPHK aircraft struck Tumbunma Baba in the Tumbuns at 06:05 on 11 September after confirmatory ISR on 9 September identified structures and logistics hubs during a reported high-profile meeting; military sources assess about 18 killed and 85 per cent effect, and recommend further interdiction — single-chain (Zagazola, republished by Vanguard), casualty figure unverified. In the North West, local sources describe five military aircraft operating over Yankuzo, Tsafe LGA, on the evening of 7 September under Op FANSAN YAMMA, killing several fighters of the Ado Aleru network including a named lieutenant, Isiya Gurgu — unverified. Separately the Nigeria Police Air Wing reports sustained aerial operations around Karaduwa, Matazu, Dutsin-Ma, Dan Musa and Yankuzo in Katsina and the Jangebe axis of Zamfara: nine killed, camps destroyed, three PKM machine guns, three AK-47s and a rocket launcher with warheads recovered. Two readings: the ISR-to-strike cycle over the lake is running at about 48 hours and the Tumbuns are ISWAP’s domain, outside any ceasefire; and at least three air operators are now working the same North West airspace — NAF under OPFY, Army/joint assets, and the Police Air Wing — which is a deconfliction question in its own right.
  • Command change in the theatre we support: Maj Gen I. A. Ajose is the new OPHK Theatre Commander (9 September), and a US C-17 delivered FMS equipment to the NAF at Lagos and Kainji. The Army’s 9 September repositioning moves Maj Gen I. A. Ajose from AHQ Department of Army Operations to Theatre Command JTF North East, replacing Maj Gen A. E. Abubakar, who goes to the Army Heritage and Future Centre. In the same list Brig Gen M. S. Sule takes 27 Task Force Brigade (the Biu formation that foiled the Buni Gari–Buratai ambush this week), Brig Gen I. Sule takes 6 Division Garrison in Port Harcourt, and Brig Gen M. S. Adamu commands a newly established 8 Brigade whose location the release does not state. Expect the usual changeover effects over the next month: a commander’s battlefield circulation to FOBs (predictable, high-value movements that need air support), a review of standing tasking and strike authorities, and an early emphasis on visible results. Separately, AFRICOM announced on 10–11 September that a USAF C-17 delivered military equipment to the Nigerian Air Force at Lagos and Kainji under an existing foreign military sales case; Kainji is where the US advisory element and the reported MQ-9 presence sit, so the deconfliction picture there is unchanged but busier.
  • Fifteen weeks with nothing employed against aircraft — but MSR devices and direct fire on fuel tanks are the ground-side lessons, and Niamey purged its army chief. No enemy drone use, no MANPADS, no GPS or EW interference and no aircraft or UAV losses, verified again by search this week; the Kadiya anti-aircraft rounds remain uncorroborated. What did happen: two command-detonated IEDs were initiated against a patrol on the Mairari–Gajiram main supply route on 5 September (no casualties, brigade garrison reinforced, enemy withdrew); a device was found and blown on the Damboa–Bulabulin–Maiduguri road; and in Sokoto simultaneous night attacks on FOB Tidibale and the village beside it (00:10, 6 September, Isa LGA) wounded one soldier and burned out two gun trucks after direct fire into their fuel tanks. Fuel-laden vehicles at a FOB under night attack burned; aircraft on a FOB pad or at a forward refuel point are the same target set. Regionally, Niger removed its armed forces chief Gen Moussa Salaou Barmou on 11 September, promoted Gen Mamane Sani Kiaou in his place, and made the Base 101 commander who stayed loyal during the mutiny deputy chief of staff — a regime-security reshuffle that will not improve cross-border coordination on the Nigeria–Niger seam. Mali lost the Dioura position in Mopti Region the same week.

2. North East Developments

Window note: the shape of the week changed again. The base attack returned (Pulka), the visible air campaign returned (Tumbunma Baba, Pulka), and the enemy’s roadside campaign continued on the MSRs south and north of Maiduguri. Underneath all of it is the ceasefire report, which if accurate has been quietly shaping the theatre since June. The theatre’s new commander inherits all three.

Pulka, 7 September — night infiltration attempt, air-supported defence

Detail as at Section 1. Two points worth extracting. First, this was an infiltration attempt rather than a mass assault — the security report describes an attempt to get inside the perimeter, not a Kadiya-style gun-truck attack — and the damage to three MRAP tyres suggests fire directed at the vehicles rather than the walls. Second, the official statement’s emphasis on the air component (ISR for situational awareness, then strikes on ‘hostile elements’ attempting to escape) is a statement of doctrine as much as a report of events: a base under attack at night will get an ISR platform first and a strike on the exfil second. Crews should assume the enemy has drawn the same conclusion and will plan the withdrawal route with that in mind. Gwoza LGA has been the week’s busiest axis for people coming out of the hills as well — see the escapes and surrenders below.

The reported ceasefire — who is in it, who is not, and what to ask

The New Humanitarian’s account describes JAS as led by Ibrahim Bakura Doro from the islands of northern Lake Chad, with three subordinate factions that have sworn allegiance to him: the Ali Ngulde faction in the Mandara mountains (which carried out the Ngoshe kidnapping), Darul Gazwa under Alhai Kale in south-eastern Borno, and the Sadiku group in north-central Nigeria. The truce is said to have been negotiated through the Office of the National Security Adviser and the DSS, to have started in June with the Ngoshe release, and to be a likely candidate for a 40-day extension. Its stability depends on whether Bakura can hold the other three commanders to it; a previous armistice in 2022–23 collapsed. The security establishment’s hoped-for end-state is described as ‘do a deal with one, to fight the other’ — an alignment with Bakura against ISWAP, which is the more lethal, better-funded and IS-Central-controlled adversary. For this brief the operational questions are concrete: which grid squares and which groups are covered; whether OPHK strike authorities have been adjusted; and how the theatre wants a JAS group observed moving in the Mandara belt handled. None of that is in open sources. The reported warning that JAS may be moving equipment into the Mandara mountains under the truce is the single most useful sentence in the piece for an ISR tasker.

Tumbunma Baba, 11 September — the lake interdiction

Detail as at Section 1. The timeline is the useful part: ISR on 9 September identified structures and logistics hubs, a reported high-profile meeting provided the trigger, and the strike went in at 06:05 on 11 September. The assessment of ‘85 per cent’ with ‘further air interdiction recommended’ means a re-attack on the Tumbuns is likely inside the next fortnight. This is ISWAP territory and unaffected by any JAS truce. Note that no independent chain corroborates the 18 figure.

The MSRs — devices and ambushes

  • Mairari–Gajiram MSR, 5 September, 09:45. A patrol with CJTF encountered two command-detonated IEDs at separate points; both were initiated as the troops approached, with no casualties, and the enemy was engaged from their firing positions until the brigade garrison, led by its commander, reinforced. Two devices, two locations, both command-initiated: a prepared engagement area on a supply route in northern Borno, not an opportunistic emplacement.
  • Damboa–Bulabulin–Maiduguri road. Troops found and safely detonated an IED planted on the road in Damboa LGA (reported 8 September). Twenty-five Brigade separately arrested a suspected foreign national on the Azir–Damboa road.
  • Buni Gari–Buratai, Biu LGA. 27 Task Force Brigade foiled an attempted Boko Haram ambush on the road and dismantled makeshift camps along the axis (reported 7–8 September). This is the formation that changes commander this week.

Mandara Mountains — the hills are leaking people

  • Escapes and rescues in Gwoza. Two minors aged 14 and 10 who had been living with fighters escaped from an enclave in the Mandara Mountains and surrendered to troops; a woman and her daughter escaped a camp in the same range and were intercepted by 82 Division Task Force Battalion; eight abductees including several children were rescued in two operations on the Gwoza East corridor; four family members of fighters, including an eight-month-old infant, surrendered to 192 Battalion (Mechanised) on the Gwoza–Limankara road on 7 September; and a suspected ISWAP member with a gunshot wound was arrested loitering near a location referred to as Papa 7. Whether this is the effect of pressure, of the truce, or of hunger, the direction of travel is out of the hills.
  • A surrender through the Chadian Defence Forces. 152 Task Force Battalion at Banki received a suspected terrorist who surrendered to Chadian forces and was handed across the border (reported 10 September) — a small but useful data point that the Chadian side of the seam is functioning as a channel.
  • Kaga. A 20-year-old man who escaped after more than three months in captivity was intercepted by troops in Kaga LGA.

Counter-network fight — logistics, informants, cash

The week’s arrests describe a supply system rather than a raiding party: two suspected collaborators with ₦3.65 million in cash in Monguno; a suspected ISWAP informant in Monguno with an identity card and drugs; three logistics suppliers in Konduga with food, household goods, bicycles and phones; two more with a large consignment of motorcycle spare parts (159 Battalion); a 22-year-old supplier and informant operating around Mada; a 20-year-old fighter who returned to Kukawa town after a year with groups on the Lake Chad axis; three drug peddlers with 92 parcels of cannabis hidden in television cartons on the Maiduguri–Mafa road; and, worth a second look, 233 Tank Battalion’s interception of a vehicle carrying eleven empty CNG cylinders and an open-loop conversion kit without clearance. Pressure vessels and gas-conversion hardware moving without paperwork in this theatre are on the IED-precursor watch-list whether or not this case turns out to be commercial.

Adamawa

247 Reconnaissance Battalion intercepted a suspected logistics supplier with 64 rolls of clothing material at a stop-and-search point (reported 8 September), and troops arrested six members of a kidnapping syndicate operating in Song LGA, recovering a Dane gun (13 September). No new camp or contact reported on the Hong/Mandara-fringe axis this window.

Schools thread — day 122 and day 77

No release, negotiation or proof-of-life reporting surfaced this window for the Mussa pupils (taken 15 May) or the 36 Lassa students and one staff member (taken 29 June). The ceasefire reporting is notable for what it does not say: it links the truce to the Ngoshe release and to a payment, and makes no mention of the schoolchildren. If the truce is real and the Ali Ngulde faction is inside it, the Lassa cohort — taken from Askira-Uba, the same broad belt — is the obvious next test of what the arrangement buys.

3. Air / ISR & Force-Protection Watch Items

  • Night base-defence tasking is the live pattern. Pulka shows the sequence: infiltration attempt around 22:00, ISR overhead, ground reinforcement, strike on the withdrawal. Plan for it — night ISR on call for the Gwoza/Mandara and Kukawa FOB belts, and a strike package that can be cued onto an exfil route rather than the base itself. Assume the enemy expects this and may site an ambush or a device on the route it wants you to think it is using.
  • The ceasefire is a targeting-policy question that nobody has answered publicly. If a truce with Bakura-aligned JAS exists, the theatre has either adjusted strike authorities in the Mandara/Gwoza/Ngoshe belt or it has not; either answer matters to a crew asked to engage a group of armed men there. Ask through the chain; do not infer from the absence of guidance. Watch also for the ‘equipment into the Mandara mountains’ indicator: unusual pack-animal, motorcycle or foot traffic into the hills under a lull is precisely what an ISR platform on that axis should be looking for.
  • Lake Chad interdiction cycle: ISR on day one, strike on day three, re-attack likely. Tumbunma Baba was found on 9 September and struck on 11 September; the assessment recommends further interdiction. Expect the Tumbuns to be re-attacked within the fortnight, and expect ISWAP to have moved what it could in the interval. Remember that ISWAP’s bases have shelters and that the group is, by its own communities’ account, ‘only nervous about the air force bombing’ — which is also an incentive to develop a counter.
  • North West airspace has at least three air operators in it. NAF under Op FANSAN YAMMA (the five-aircraft package reported over Yankuzo on 7 September, if accurate, is a large one), Army and joint assets, and the Nigeria Police Air Wing running its own surveillance, troop insertion, air cover and interdiction missions across Katsina and Zamfara. Any of our tasking into the North West — or transits across it — needs the Police Air Wing in the deconfliction picture, not just the NAF. The rocket launcher with warheads recovered in Katsina is an RPG-class weapon and a standing low-level threat to rotary aircraft in the OPFY area.
  • Fuel on the ground is the target the enemy hit this week. At FOB Tidibale two gun trucks burned out after direct fire into their fuel tanks during a night attack. The rotary-wing equivalent is a fuelled airframe on a FOB pad, a forward arming and refuelling point, or a bowser. Where we operate from or through austere FOBs at night, fuel dispersal, berming and the distance between the pad and the perimeter are worth a conversation with the ground commander.
  • Command changeover — expect movement, and expect a review of tasking. A new theatre commander will circulate to sectors and FOBs early; those are predictable, announced VIP movements of the kind that drew the Kareto–Damasak convoy attack, and they will draw air-support tasking. Expect also a re-look at strike authorities, ISR priorities and the balance between the Lake Chad, Sambisa and Mandara axes, and an early appetite for demonstrable results. Note the 27 Task Force Brigade change at Biu and the new 6 Division Garrison commander in Port Harcourt if we route through either.
  • Threat systems: nothing employed — fifteen weeks. No enemy drone use, no MANPADS, no GPS or EW interference, no aircraft or UAV losses. The last documented armed-drone use against a Nigerian base remains Sabon Gari, Damboa (late January 2026); the last confirmed airframe loss remains the 2 January CH-4 (technical failure, Kontagora). The Kadiya anti-aircraft rounds (29 August, single-source) remain uncorroborated — keep the indicator open. Standing background is unchanged: the February 2026 reporting of about 35 drones moved to ISWAP through the Lake Chad corridor, and ISWAP’s demonstrated appetite for technology (ADF this month on AI-assisted attack planning).
  • US dimension — an FMS delivery, no new kinetic activity, and a political undertow. AFRICOM’s C-17 delivery to the NAF at Lagos and Kainji is the first visible US materiel movement in some weeks; DICON’s Director General, Maj Gen Babatunde Alaya, appeared on an NDIA panel with AFRICOM leaders on 8–10 September. No new AFRICOM strike announcements. Standing posture for deconfliction purposes is unchanged (about 200 US personnel since 10 February 2026 at Maiduguri, Kainji and Bauchi; MQ-9 Reapers reported; a US–Nigeria fusion cell; Nigerian DHQ says the Americans are in non-combat roles). The undertow is that Washington is helping Abuja fight a group Abuja may be quietly in a truce with; if the ceasefire reporting hardens, expect it to surface in the congressional debate.
  • Regional airspace: Niger is reorganising for regime security, not for counter-terrorism. Barmou out, Kiaou in as armed forces chief, Gen Abdourahmane Abou Zataka up to army chief of staff, and the Base 101 commander who held the airport base for the regime, Gen Ismael K. Sidi Omar, promoted to deputy chief of staff (RFI via Al Jazeera). Zagazola’s analysis reads it as the first step of a wider loyalty-driven restructuring that will degrade the special-operations capability Barmou built and push JNIM/ISSP pressure toward Sokoto, Kebbi, Zamfara, Katsina, Jigawa and Yobe. Continue to assume unpredictable Nigerien airspace management and no functioning cross-border deconfliction. Mali’s loss of Dioura (Ténenkou, Mopti) and the Sudan war’s spill into Chad are the wider context: every neighbour on the northern arc is looking inward.
  • Information environment: the ceasefire story is itself the week’s main information event. Expect denial, counter-leak and political use of it as the 2027 campaign warms up — Senator Buba’s counter-complaint over banditry allegations and the Army’s denial that troops abducted clerics from Senator Yari’s residence in Zamfara are the same phenomenon at state level. The operating rule stands: treat any loss, shoot-down, casualty or atrocity claim involving air assets as unverified until confirmed through the chain, and expect claims to be timed for political effect.

M.   • South East — a quiet week in reporting; levels hold. No IED, camp raid or contact was reported inside the window in Anambra, Imo, Abia, Enugu or Ebonyi; Imo and Anambra hold 3 on the August–early-September pattern. A widely surfaced NAN item on NAF strikes against IPOB/ESN hideouts at Mother Valley and Aku Ihube is from September 2023 and was excluded.

Edo goes 1→2.   • Regional and political weather. Niger’s command reshuffle (Sections 1 and 3); Mali’s loss of Dioura; Zagazola’s analysis that the Sudan war is spilling into Chad and that Nigeria is now the buffer on the eastern arc — relevant to how much attention Chad’s MNJTF sector will get. Domestically: an investigation opened into the death of a senior NCO after a shooting at Kuje custodial centre in the FCT; the Correctional Service and Army Intelligence agreed to deepen custodial-security cooperation; the federal government began operationalising 112 as the national emergency number.
